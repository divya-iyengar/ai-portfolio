## Summary

As a Graduate Research Assistant in Georgia Tech's Dynamic Adaptive Robotic Technologies (DART) Lab, I conducted robotics research focused on safe navigation, trajectory planning, wearable robotics, and human-centered autonomous systems.

My research was performed within a broader collaboration involving DART Lab and the Exoskeleton and Prosthetic Intelligent Controls (EPIC) Lab, where researchers investigated applications of robotics across aerospace, ground robotics, wearable systems, and assistive technologies.

My primary research focused on developing human-centric navigation algorithms that combined motion planning and obstacle avoidance techniques to improve safety in complex, unstructured environments. Beyond my own research, I contributed to related wearable robotics projects, supported doctoral research efforts, mentored undergraduate researchers, led robotics education initiatives, and served as Head Teaching Assistant for an upper-level robotics course.

This experience combined algorithm development, software engineering, experimental design, data analysis, technical communication, and research leadership.

# Research Contributions

## Human-Centric Motion Planning and Safe Navigation

My primary research focused on improving human safety in environments where workers may interact with heavy machinery, moving objects, or other hazards.

I investigated how robotics-based motion planning approaches could be adapted to provide humans with intelligent navigation guidance rather than directly controlling their movement. This required combining concepts from autonomous navigation, trajectory planning, human motion modeling, and virtual reality-based experimentation.

I developed software tools and experimental pipelines to evaluate human-centered navigation strategies, including:

- Implementing motion planning algorithms for safe navigation around static and dynamic obstacles
- Developing simulation environments for human-in-the-loop experiments
- Processing and analyzing experimental data collected through virtual reality systems
- Evaluating navigation performance through quantitative metrics
- Designing experiments to compare algorithm performance against baseline conditions

This research resulted in a master's thesis investigating integrated global and local planning approaches for human navigation assistance.

# Collaboration with PhD Researchers

Within the DART and EPIC Lab collaboration, I worked alongside doctoral researchers whose projects focused on wearable robotics, exoskeleton control, and human-robot interaction.

I supported multiple research efforts by contributing experimental development, data collection, analysis, and engineering support.

For a hip exoskeleton research project, I supported experiments investigating metabolic cost reduction and the effectiveness of a high-torque quasi-direct drive hip exoskeleton. I assisted with experimental execution, subject testing workflows, and analysis of collected data.

For a human navigation assistance project using a lower-limb exoskeleton, I contributed to research exploring force-feedback strategies for guiding users around environmental hazards. This included supporting early experimental design, force-feedback tuning investigations, and evaluation of human responses.

These collaborations contributed to two peer-reviewed journal publications:

- "Design and validation of a versatile high torque quasidirect drive hip exoskeleton"
- "Enhancing human navigation ability using force-feedback from a lower-limb exoskeleton"

# Challenges

A major challenge of graduate research was translating theoretical robotics algorithms into systems that could be evaluated with real users. Unlike purely autonomous systems, human-centered robotics requires consideration of human behavior, comfort, perception, and usability.

Developing effective experiments required balancing algorithmic performance with practical constraints such as human response variability, simulation realism, and experimental repeatability.

Another challenge was managing multiple responsibilities simultaneously, including independent research, collaboration with doctoral researchers, mentoring undergraduate students, teaching, and leading educational programs.

# Lessons Learned

My graduate research experience reinforced that successful robotics systems require more than strong algorithms. Real-world impact depends on connecting technical development with human needs, experimental validation, and clear communication.

I learned how to take ambiguous research questions, break them into measurable engineering problems, develop solutions iteratively, and validate results through structured experimentation.

Beyond technical skills, mentoring students, teaching robotics concepts, and leading research initiatives strengthened my ability to communicate complex ideas, guide teams, and collaborate across different levels of technical expertise.
